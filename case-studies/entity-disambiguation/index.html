Entity Disambiguation

On 15 July, Google's AI summary answered a question about my consultancy by describing a planning firm in England. The two businesses share four letters and nothing else. Fixing it took no new content, because the problem was never what my pages said. Nothing in them said which organization was speaking.

What the summary got wrong

Pigasus Group is my consultancy, in Boone, North Carolina. Pegasus Planning Group is a planning consultancy in the United Kingdom. Google's AI summary treated them as one business, and described the British one.

The mix-up was not the worst of the baseline. That same first measurement found the site ranking around 22nd for its own name, and a national prompt set covering the work it does returned zero mentions. That date lives at the top of the schema module, because a fix with no incident attached gets refactored away by the next person who cannot see why it exists.

Five pages, five anonymous organizations

The site had JSON-LD on every page, which was the trap. Each page minted its own organization inline, so the graph held a scatter of nameless organizations sharing one string, with nothing joining them to each other or to any fact outside the site.

The service pages made it concrete. The industries hub and its four industry pages each declared a professional-service node whose provider was an inline organization with no identifier, all five claimed the homepage as their own URL, and none carried a description, the exact field an assistant quotes when somebody asks what a business does for restaurants. A machine has no reason to prefer five nameless entities to a British firm with a public record.

Anchors, and an asymmetry I left alone

The repair is two constants. One identifier resolves the organization at /#organization, another resolves me at /about#david-luther, and every Service, BlogPosting, and BreadcrumbList now points at those instead of declaring a new node. On the five service pages the provider became a bare reference to that identifier, the URLs became the pages they describe, and each description now comes from the constant that page feeds its meta description, so the two cannot drift.

What stayed uneven is deliberate. The Person and Organization nodes carry different external corroboration, because the local chamber of commerce listing is a person listing rather than a business one. It vouches for me, and the organization's own profiles vouch for the organization. The code carries a dated instruction not to reconcile the two lists, which reads like an inconsistency and is the opposite of one. Tidying them to match would have handed the assistant a person and a company vouching for each other in a loop.

Markup that outlived its payoff

Google retired FAQ rich results on 7 May 2026, pulled the Search Console report and Rich Results Test support in June, de-published the documentation on 15 June, and scheduled the API data to follow in August. The FAQ markup on the site stayed where it was.

It earns no rich result and never will again. It ships because FAQPage is still valid schema.org and consumers other than Google read structured data, the same bet an llms.txt file and an identifier graph already make. Assistants quote rendered text rather than JSON-LD, so the visible questions feed the answer and the markup is a cheap byproduct of writing them. That is the playbook's structured-data step as a live decision rather than a recommendation.

Two similar names do not make an assistant curious. They make it hedge, and the hedge is where a brand goes missing.

What the re-check proved, and what it didn't

Three days after the fixes shipped, the branded AI Overview described the business accurately. Right firm, right town, right services, and the English consultancy gone from the answer.

Three days is faster than these systems usually re-learn anything, so I recorded it as directional rather than settled and put the question set back on a schedule instead of writing it up as a win. The same standard later disqualified my own sitemap fix on a different site. Timing plus a plausible mechanism is a hypothesis, and one that flatters the person holding it deserves more suspicion rather than less.

The same check, twice more

One incident stays an anecdote until it changes how other systems work. This one became a screening step in two of them.

In the enterprise measurement program I run, brand ambiguity gets screened before a visibility report is configured, rather than after its numbers are built and quoted in a deliverable. A maintained ambiguity list is checked first, anything absent goes to a judgment pass that scores severity and names the competing senses, and a post-pull noise audit catches the rest. Without it, a competitor whose brand name is an ordinary English noun collects share of voice from every query about the noun, and the chart reads like a competitive finding when it is an artifact.

In my consultancy's own assessment engine, a collision detector runs against captured listing data, and its fixture is four real-world brand collisions plus one synthetic negative control built from the same brand root in an unrelated industry in a different city. The control is the point, because a detector that fires on everything proves nothing.

What I still cannot tell you is whether the correction survives the next model refresh, or whether an assistant that once merged the two names keeps some residue of it. The question set comes around on its schedule, and I find out then.

Build notes. The schema module and its dated comments are my own consultancy's code, and the starting figures were published first-person on the Pigasus site at the time, embarrassing numbers included. Everything from the day job sits at this site's usual altitude, with no figures or tooling names, and the collision fixture is described by shape rather than by the brands inside it.
