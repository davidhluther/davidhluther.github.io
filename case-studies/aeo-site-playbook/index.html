The AEO Site-Readiness Playbook

Making a site legible to AI systems is an infrastructure layer with a deployment order. Here's the order, why it's the order, and a live deployment you're reading right now.

Step 1: Crawler access, audited rather than assumed

Before any optimization, can AI systems fetch your pages at all? Audit robots directives for AI crawler user agents specifically, because wildcard defaults were written for a different era's crawlers. At Corpay this audit found an existing advantage worth defending, with explicit allow rules where much of the competitive set either blocked AI agents or relied on defaults, so the job became tightening crawl delays and adding newly emerged agents as they appear. On this site, robots.txt welcomes GPTBot, ClaudeBot, PerplexityBot, and Google-Extended by name.

Access is not the same as discovery, and the two fail differently. A crawler that is allowed in still has to learn a page exists, which is a separate problem with its own diagnosis. I worked one of those end to end on my weather site after 34 new pages went unfound.

Step 2: Structured data in dependency order

The audit that kicked off Corpay's deployment found a large site with no machine-readable structured data anywhere. That's common, and it's quietly expensive, since AI systems could reach the content but had nothing structured to extract. The deployment discipline mattered as much as the markup. Money pages went first, sequenced by impression volume rather than by ease. Page types got their appropriate entities, plus FAQ markup whose answers must match visible on-page content. And defects got fixed before becoming machine-readable, because rich results bake in whatever's there. One page's schema deployment waited on an unrelated title-tag fix for exactly that reason. Rating markup got checked against live review counts, and an offer block that would have misstated a price got removed. Structured data amplifies what's already there, including the errors.

Then it scaled, moving from a hand-deployed priority set into the content pipeline as a production step. This site carries Person and ProfilePage schema on the home page and TechArticle schema on every case study, all in JSON-LD.

Schema is entity infrastructure before it's rich-result decoration, and I have the incident to prove it. In July, Google's AI summary confidently blended my consultancy, Pigasus Group, with a UK planning consultancy called Pegasus, one letter and an ocean away. The fix was identity rather than volume. Every schema node on the Pigasus site now carries a stable @id anchored to one organization node, so a machine assembling an answer resolves against an unambiguous graph instead of two similar strings. FAQ schema on the same site stayed deployed after Google retired FAQ rich results, a call made in writing at the time, because the classic-SEO payoff was never the point. Assistants still extract from it.

Step 3: llms.txt as a citation-shaping asset

An llms.txt file usually gets described as a sitemap for AI, which undersells it. Schema makes individual pages extractable, while llms.txt makes the organization legible, and it's the one place you can tell AI systems how to talk about you. The Corpay deployment carried canonical product descriptions in the company's own framing, an explicit target-audience section, differentiators, and a citation-preferences block stating how the brand should be attributed. For a company whose legacy and acquired sub-brand names still circulate in training data, that block addresses an observable attribution problem directly.

The honest framing, recorded at deployment time, was that no AI platform had officially confirmed reading these files, adoption was accelerating anyway, and publishing one costs minutes. It shipped ahead of every tracked competitor. This site's own llms.txt follows the pattern, citation preferences included.

I'll note the counterexample myself, since honesty is the house style. On my own weather site I evaluated llms.txt and declined it, and it still doesn't have one. The reason is worth stating precisely, because that site is not short of machine-readable surface, as the next step but one shows. llms.txt earns its place by making an organization legible, meaning the product lines, the framing, and the attribution problems worth heading off. A one-person satirical weather site has none of that to disambiguate. What it had to offer machines was data, so it published the data instead. The playbook is a decision framework, and sometimes the decision is no.

Step 4: Semantic HTML and answer-ready copy

Retrieval systems chunk pages (here's how, mechanically), and chunks travel without their surroundings. Concrete writing rules follow. Headings state the claim, because a heading is the chunk's label in the index. Meta descriptions read as complete, citable sentences about what the page establishes. Sections stand alone, since a paragraph that depends on three paragraphs of prior context loses the citation to one that doesn't. And the markup uses real semantics, meaning article, nav, heading hierarchy, and lists that are lists. Extraction quality follows markup quality.

Step 5: Gate the PDF, never the answers

Lead-gen instinct says gate the good stuff, and for answer engines that instinct is fatal. The publish shape that works: the designed whitepaper PDF stays behind the form, while the pillar page, glossary, answer blocks, and chart pack stay ungated and crawlable. Gating the canonical answer surface is permanently off the table, because a page an assistant can't retrieve is a page that doesn't exist to it.

Two sequencing rules travel with this. Measurement prompts enter the tracking pool before publication, since a post-publication baseline can't be reconstructed, and indexation on the engine that feeds assistant retrieval gets treated as a prerequisite rather than a traffic channel, with a measured publish-to-first-citation lag distribution setting the evaluation windows. You can't grade content on a timeline the retrieval layer never agreed to.

Step 6: Publish the data itself, licensed

The previous step says don't gate the answers. The step past it is to hand over the underlying data, on terms a machine can read, and my weather site is where I did that rather than only recommending it. Its accuracy record ships as Dataset JSON-LD on the scoreboard and on every town page, carrying a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 license URL in the markup, so the licence travels with the structured data instead of sitting in a footer nobody parses.

Underneath that sits a public JSON API of five endpoints covering the consensus forecast, today's verdict, the season scoreboard, a single day's result, and the town registry. Every response is stamped with license, attribution, and source fields, so a system that picks up one payload three hops from the origin still carries the credit terms with it. Fetch any of them and you can check that yourself. There are RSS feeds per town as well, four each, and an embeddable widget for anyone who wants the forecast on their own page.

Two decisions in there are the ones I would defend in an interview. The widget is server-rendered from data already committed to the repo, makes no view-time requests, and has every analytics script deliberately suppressed, because it runs on other people's pages and an embed should cost the host one iframe and no third-party calls. Its footer attribution doubles as the required backlink, which is what makes the licence self-enforcing rather than aspirational.

The second is a gate. A town only enters the API once it has crossed the same track-record threshold the site uses on its own pages, so a thin sample is never dressed up as a dataset. That restraint is the difference between publishing data and publishing volume, and it is the same instinct as the scoring rubric's refusal to rank a source before it has enough scored days to rank. Publishing eighteen towns, each with its own coordinates, its own forecasts, and its own graded record, is only worth something if each one is genuinely distinct. That site's own published analysis of the paid local incumbent found 66 listed locations resolving to three distinct written forecasts, which is the failure mode this discipline exists to avoid.

None of it is a claim you have to take on trust. The repo is public, the site is live, and 581 tests run against the pipeline that produces all of it.

Step 7: Weight discipline

This site is HTML, one stylesheet, and six subsetted font files. No JavaScript, no tracking, no framework. Every technique above works better when the page is trivially fetchable and parseable. View source on any page here and you're looking at exactly what a crawler gets.

The proof structure

The playbook's claims are checkable against this site in about two minutes, through robots.txt, llms.txt, view-source on any page, and sitemap.xml. Step 6 is checkable on a different site, which is the point of citing it, since its API and its licensed markup are live and inspectable without my involvement. What this site can't demonstrate is the enterprise half, meaning the sequencing politics, the verification gates, and the pipeline integration, which is why the Corpay methodology sits alongside the worked example.

A playbook that doesn't run on its author's own site is a hypothesis.
