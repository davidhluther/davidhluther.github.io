The AEO site-readiness playbook (this site runs it)

Making a site legible to AI systems is an infrastructure layer with a deployment order, not a content project. Here's the order, why it's the order, and a live deployment you're reading right now.

Step 1: Crawler access, audited rather than assumed

Before any optimization: can AI systems fetch your pages at all? Audit robots directives for AI crawler user agents specifically, because wildcard defaults were written for a different era's crawlers. At Corpay this audit found an existing advantage worth defending: explicit allow rules where much of the competitive set either blocked AI agents or relied on defaults, so the job became tightening crawl delays and adding newly emerged agents as they appear. On this site, robots.txt explicitly welcomes GPTBot, ClaudeBot, PerplexityBot, and Google-Extended.

Step 2: Structured data on the pages that matter, in dependency order

The audit that kicked off Corpay's deployment found a large site with no machine-readable structured data anywhere, which is common and quietly expensive: AI systems could reach the content but had nothing structured to extract. The deployment discipline mattered as much as the markup:

  • Money pages first, sequenced by impression volume and opportunity, not by ease.
  • Page-type-appropriate types: organization and product/service entities, plus FAQ markup whose answers must match visible on-page content.
  • Fix defects before making them machine-readable. A page with a broken title tag gets the title fixed before schema ships, because rich results bake in whatever's there.
  • Verify any claim you're about to encode as structured fact. Rating markup gets checked against live review counts; an offer block that would misstate a price gets removed. Structured data amplifies what's already there, including the errors.
  • Then scale it: from a hand-deployed priority set into the content pipeline as a production step.

This site carries Person and ProfilePage schema on the home page and TechArticle schema on every case study, all in JSON-LD.

Step 3: llms.txt as a citation-shaping asset

An llms.txt file is usually described as a sitemap for AI. That undersells it. Schema makes individual pages extractable; llms.txt makes the organization legible, and it's the one place you can tell AI systems how to talk about you. The Corpay deployment carried canonical product descriptions in the company's own framing, an explicit target-audience section so AI systems match the brand to the right queries, differentiators so competitive answers have source material, and a citation-preferences block stating how the brand should be attributed. For a company whose legacy and acquired sub-brand names still circulate in training data, that block is a direct fix for an observable attribution problem.

The honest framing, recorded at deployment time: no AI platform had officially confirmed reading these files, adoption was accelerating anyway, and the cost of publishing one is minutes. It shipped ahead of every tracked competitor. This site's own llms.txt follows the same pattern, citation preferences included.

Step 4: Semantic HTML and answer-ready copy

Retrieval systems chunk pages (see how, mechanically), and chunks travel without their surroundings. That implies concrete writing rules:

  • Headings that state the claim, not the vibe, because a heading is the chunk's label in the index.
  • Meta descriptions written as answer-ready summaries: a complete, citable sentence about what the page establishes, not a teaser.
  • Self-contained sections. A paragraph that depends on three paragraphs of prior context loses the citation to one that stands alone.
  • Real HTML semantics: article, nav, heading hierarchy, lists that are lists. Extraction quality follows markup quality.

Step 5: Weight discipline

This site is plain HTML and one stylesheet. No framework, no JavaScript, no fonts to download, no tracking. That's not asceticism; it's the observation that every technique above works better when the page is trivially fetchable and parseable. View source on any page here and you're looking at exactly what a crawler gets.

The proof structure

The playbook's claims are checkable against this site in about two minutes: robots.txt for crawler policy, llms.txt for the organization file, view-source on any page for JSON-LD and semantic markup, and sitemap.xml for clean URLs.

A playbook that doesn't run on its author's own site is a hypothesis.
